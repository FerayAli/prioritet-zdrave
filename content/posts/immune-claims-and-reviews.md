## Why this matters

Marketing often promises oils that “boost immunity.” Peer-reviewed work more often reports **changes in specific immune parameters** (cytokines, phagocytosis, antibody titers) in **cell, animal, or limited human** studies—not protection from infection in real life.

## What researchers did

Narrative reviews searched PubMed for essential oils described as immunomodulatory or immunostimulatory, cataloging in vitro, in vivo, and human measurements. Oils such as **eucalyptus**, ginger, and thyme appear across multiple experimental papers.

## Main findings

- Reviews list **mixed and sometimes contradictory** results: some oils increase certain cytokines or phagocytic activity in models; others show anti-inflammatory profiles.
- Human trials are **fewer** than bench studies and often use undefined aromatherapy protocols.
- Forest-bathing and environmental volatile organic compound exposure are discussed separately from bottle essential oils.

## What this does not prove

Improved marker in a mouse model ≠ preventing colds in your household. Diffusing eucalyptus or tea tree is not a vaccine substitute or cancer immunotherapy.

## Practical context

[Eucalyptus](/book/eucalyptus) and [tea tree](/book/tea-tree) have clearer evidence in **respiratory comfort and topical antimicrobial** contexts than vague “immune boost” slogans.
