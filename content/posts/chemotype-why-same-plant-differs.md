## Why this matters

Essential oils are not one fixed recipe per plant. Growers, season, and genetics can shift which molecules dominate. Research on *Lippia alba*—a shrub used in traditional medicine and sometimes compared with lemongrass-scented herbs—shows how a **citral-rich chemotype** can score strongly in antioxidant and enzyme-inhibition tests while a carvone-rich chemotype behaves differently. That gap between “same species” and “different chemistry” is why two bottles with similar names may not match what you read in a study.

## What researchers did

Dutta et al. (2026) extracted leaf essential oil from *Lippia alba*, analyzed it with GC-MS, and ran in vitro assays: DPPH and ABTS antioxidant tests, trypsin inhibition (used here as an anti-inflammatory proxy), acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibition, and tyrosinase inhibition. They also ran molecular docking against COX-2 and human AChE. This was **bench science**, not a clinical trial in people.

Earlier work on three *L. alba* chemotypes (citral, carvone, linalool) combined anticholinesterase assays with docking to compare major constituents against AChE.

## Main findings

- The 2026 leaf oil profile was **citral-oriented** (geranial about 26% of identified compounds) with 26 compounds reported overall.
- Antioxidant activity: reported IC~50~ values about **10.7 µg/mL (ABTS)** and **15.7 µg/mL (DPPH)** in that study’s conditions.
- Trypsin inhibition IC~50~ about **16.1 µg/mL**; AChE inhibition IC~50~ about **10.1 µg/mL**; tyrosinase inhibition IC~50~ about **11.2 µg/mL** in the same paper.
- Docking suggested some constituents (e.g. cubebol) had favorable calculated binding to AChE compared with a reference ligand—**computational only**, not proof of effect in the body.
- Chemotype comparisons elsewhere show **different dominant terpenes** (citral vs carvone vs linalool) and different AChE inhibition potencies between chemotypes.

## What this does not prove

In vitro IC~50~ values and docking scores do **not** tell you that diffusing or applying a retail lemongrass or verbena-scented oil will treat inflammation, memory loss, or skin pigmentation. Species names on labels may not match *L. alba* at all. Dose, purity, and route in studies rarely match home use.

## Practical context

When you read a headline about “an essential oil” and brain or inflammation enzymes, ask: **which plant, which chemotype, which part of the plant, and was it tested in cells or in people?** Our [lemongrass](/book/lemongrass) page describes the oil we discuss in blends—not *Lippia alba*—but the chemotype lesson applies across the catalog.
